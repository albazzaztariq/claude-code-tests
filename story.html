How It Started

Our Story

01
The Problem

He Noticed It Every Single Day

Mohamed Abdelfatah spent years on and around wrestling mats at the highest level — first as a competitor, then as a coach at the US Olympic Training Center. And every tournament, every practice, the same thing played out: wrestlers finished their warmup, waited for their bout, and wrapped themselves in cotton.

By the time they competed, stepped off the mat, and put the robe back on, that cotton was already absorbing sweat and holding it. Clammy. Heavy. Cold. Between bouts it made keeping warm harder, not easier. It was a problem nobody had bothered to solve — because nobody had thought hard enough about it.

Mo had coached enough athletes through enough tournaments to know this wasn't a minor inconvenience. Staying warm and keeping dry between matches is part of performance. The equipment wasn't doing its job.

On the mat — where the problem was first noticed
02
The Research

All-Nighters and Academic Papers

When the idea of building a better wrestling robe took shape, a researcher got to work. Eventually leaving his job to pursue it full-time, he spent countless hours — nights, weekends, early mornings — pulling data from academic papers on textile science, moisture management, and fabric engineering.

One material kept appearing in the literature: electrospun nanofiber. A fabric structure so different from anything knitted or woven that it operates on different physics entirely — moving moisture through capillary force rather than absorbing it. The more papers he read, the clearer it became. This was the answer.

The image to the left is what that material looks like under a scanning electron microscope. Not a weave. Not a knit. A continuous web of sub-micron fibers — each one hundreds of times thinner than a human hair.

Electrospun Nanofiber — SEM Image
Fundamentally different from knits and wovens. This is what's in your robe.

03
The Build

Revision After Revision Until It Was Right

Research is one thing. A product you can hand a wrestler before a match is another. Mo and his partner spent a significant amount of time on the first prototype — not just making it functional, but making it right.

Every revision was informed by what they knew about how wrestlers actually move, warm up, and compete. The layer structure — polyester inner, electrospun nanofiber membrane, nylon outer — wasn't arrived at overnight. It was the result of going back to the drawing board repeatedly until the robe performed the way the research said it could.

No PFAS. No fluorinated chemistry. Performance built entirely into the physical structure of the fabric — because that's what the science supported, and because anything else wasn't good enough.

The Layer Decision

The three-layer system was finalized after extensive prototype testing — polyester inner for skin contact and initial wicking, nanofiber membrane for directional transport, nylon outer for protection and rapid evaporation.

No PFAS — By Design

Achieving water-resistance without fluorinated chemicals required choosing the right structure over the easy chemical shortcut. Every layer earns its performance through engineering, not coatings.

Revision After Revision

The first version wasn't the last. Each iteration was tested, critiqued, and rebuilt — until Mo was satisfied it met the standard he expected from equipment used at the highest levels of the sport.

Still Going

The Research Doesn't Stop

Having a finished product on the market doesn't mean the work is done — both Mo and his partner continue to read current research in textiles and performance apparel — tracking new developments in fiber science, coating technologies, and moisture management. TiO₂ photocatalytic coatings, next-generation wettability gradients, improved membrane architectures — these are already on the radar.

This is not a product that was built once and left to sit. It was founded on research, and research is how it stays cutting-edge. That commitment doesn't change.
